\begin{abstract}
High-resolution drone imagery is increasingly important for disaster response because it captures local scene evidence that complements satellite and street-view observations. The same resolution that makes these images useful also makes them expensive to store, move, and reconstruct in high-performance computing (HPC) workflows. This paper studies conditional diffusion compression (CDC) as a cost-aware compression and reconstruction strategy for 4K-5K drone imagery on GPU HPC systems. We do not propose a new neural compression architecture. Instead, we ask which operational controls make an existing CDC workflow practical for disaster-imagery workloads. We adapt an x-parameterized CDC implementation to NCSA DeltaAI, evaluate full-resolution drone images cropped to $5440 \times 3648$ pixels, and measure runtime, peak GPU memory, estimated bits per pixel (BPP), compression ratio, PSNR, SSIM, pixel-error statistics, and tile-boundary artifacts. Native full-image processing is feasible but expensive: at 65 denoising steps, the baseline uses about 52 GB of GPU memory and takes about 144 seconds per image. A selected $256 \times 256$ tiling configuration reduces runtime from 144.35 to 79.84 seconds per image and peak memory from 52.01 to 1.66 GB on 50 full-resolution images, while keeping scalar quality close to the no-tiling and $512 \times 512$ tiling references. Resolution reduction provides larger raw speedups, but it removes visual detail and degrades SSIM substantially at 2K and 1K settings. Checkpoint choice changes the storage-quality trade-off, while larger controlled-resolution batches and local storage staging do not improve throughput in the measured runs. The results show that CDC deployment for disaster imagery must be reported as a joint systems and image-quality problem: bitrate alone is not enough.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Drone imagery has become a practical source of post-disaster visual evidence. It can capture road blockages, roof damage, flood debris, and parcel-scale changes at a level of detail that coarser remote sensing products often cannot provide. This role is adjacent to recent disaster-imagery work that uses generative cross-view synthesis to reason from satellite to street-level views \cite{yang2026satellite} and bi-temporal street-view imagery to support hyperlocal damage assessment \cite{yang2025hyperlocal}. Together, these lines of work point to a broader need: disaster-response systems need visual data that are detailed enough for inspection, but inexpensive enough to store, move, and process repeatedly.

High-resolution drone images create a systems problem before they become a modeling problem. The images in our workflow are cropped to $5440 \times 3648$ pixels. A single 8-bit RGB frame at this resolution contains about 59.5 MB of uncompressed pixel data. A field collection with thousands of frames can therefore become a multi-hundred-gigabyte or terabyte-scale archive. In an HPC setting, the cost appears in several places: long-term storage, transfer from project filesystems to compute nodes, GPU memory pressure, queue time, and repeated decoding when analysts rerun quality checks or downstream models.

Learned image compression offers one path through this bottleneck. Autoencoder-based neural codecs and hyperprior models have shown strong rate-distortion performance \cite{balle2018hyperprior}. Diffusion models provide a different reconstruction mechanism: they can generate visually plausible details through iterative denoising \cite{ho2020ddpm}, and latent diffusion models reduce the cost of high-resolution synthesis by operating in a compact latent space \cite{rombach2022ldm}. Conditional diffusion compression (CDC) uses a diffusion decoder as part of a lossy compression system \cite{yang2022lossy}. That design is attractive for visual quality, but it introduces a practical deployment issue: diffusion decoding is iterative, and full-resolution reconstruction can be slow and memory-heavy.

This paper studies CDC as an HPC workflow rather than as a new compression model. We ask: \textit{Which runtime settings make CDC usable for large drone-imagery workloads while preserving enough visual detail for disaster-response inspection?} We focus on operational choices that a research team can control without retraining the model: denoising steps, precision, resolution, tile size, tile batch size, checkpoint choice, job parallelism, and storage placement.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item We adapt a CDC compression and reconstruction workflow to NCSA DeltaAI and define a reproducible experiment suite for high-resolution drone imagery.
\item We report the cost-quality behavior of native full-resolution processing, denoising-step reduction, resolution reduction, tiling, batch-size changes, checkpoint selection, storage placement, and parallel shards.
\item We identify $256 \times 256$ tiling as the best current runtime-memory operating point in the selected validation run, reducing peak GPU memory by 31.3$\times$ and runtime by 44.7\% relative to no tiling.
\item We show why visual QA is necessary for tiled CDC: scalar metrics are useful, but heatmaps, comparison panels, and seam checks are needed to detect spatial artifacts that would matter in disaster imagery.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Disaster Imagery and Visual Evidence}

Disaster-response imagery spans satellite, aerial, drone, street-view, and field observations. Each modality carries a different trade-off between coverage, resolution, timeliness, and interpretability. Satellite imagery provides broad coverage, but it may miss street-level details. Street-view imagery gives a ground perspective, but it may not be available immediately after an event. Yang et al. \cite{yang2026satellite} study post-disaster satellite-to-street view synthesis and emphasize the realism-fidelity tension in generative disaster imagery. Yang et al. \cite{yang2025hyperlocal} use pre- and post-disaster street-view imagery with pretrained vision models for hyperlocal damage assessment. Our work is complementary: it does not synthesize new views or classify damage directly. It asks how to compress and reconstruct high-resolution drone imagery so that such visual evidence can move through HPC workflows at lower cost.

\subsection{Learned Compression and Diffusion Reconstruction}

Learned image compression commonly optimizes a rate-distortion objective. Hyperprior models improve learned codecs by modeling latent dependencies and side information \cite{balle2018hyperprior}. CDC differs from deterministic decoder approaches because reconstruction uses a conditional diffusion model \cite{yang2022lossy}. This creates a useful quality path but also a runtime path that depends on denoising steps. Ho et al. \cite{ho2020ddpm} established diffusion probabilistic models as iterative denoising generators, and Rombach et al. \cite{rombach2022ldm} showed that latent-space diffusion can improve the efficiency of high-resolution synthesis. For our setting, the relevant question is not whether diffusion can reconstruct visually plausible images. It is whether the iterative decoder can be scheduled, tiled, and measured well enough for operational use on large drone-image collections.

\subsection{HPC Workflow Framing}

HPC image workflows often fail at practical boundaries: a model may fit on a small test image but become slow or memory-limited at native resolution. DeltaAI is built around NVIDIA GH200 Grace Hopper systems \cite{ncsa2026deltaai}, which makes it a relevant platform for evaluating GPU memory pressure and full-resolution reconstruction. We treat platform cost as part of the method. A compression setting is not useful merely because it reports a favorable BPP; it must also fit GPU memory, reconstruct fast enough, preserve relevant visual structure, and leave auditable evidence for the user.

\section{Problem Definition}

\subsection{Workflow Objective}

Let $x \in [0,1]^{H \times W \times 3}$ denote a full-resolution drone image. A CDC workflow maps $x$ to a compressed representation $z$ and reconstructs an image $\hat{x}$ through a conditional diffusion decoder. In this paper, the target image size is $H \times W = 3648 \times 5440$ after cropping. The workflow must satisfy three practical requirements:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Storage reduction}: the compressed representation should be much smaller than uncompressed RGB.
\item \textit{Feasible reconstruction}: decoding should fit GPU memory and finish within a usable time budget.
\item \textit{Inspectability}: reconstruction artifacts should be visible to reviewers through tables, heatmaps, and side-by-side comparisons.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Metrics}

We report compression ratio against uncompressed 8-bit RGB storage:
\begin{equation}
R = \frac{24}{\mathrm{BPP}},
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
where BPP is the estimated model bitrate. A BPP of 0.35 corresponds to a compression ratio of about $68.6\times$.

For a candidate configuration $\theta$, we also report runtime reduction and memory reduction relative to a baseline:
\begin{equation}
S_T(\theta) = 1 - \frac{T_{\theta}}{T_{\mathrm{base}}},
\label{eq:speedup}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
G_M(\theta) = \frac{M_{\mathrm{base}}}{M_{\theta}},
\label{eq:memorygain}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is wall time per image and $M$ is peak GPU memory. These quantities make the systems trade-off explicit. They do not replace image-quality metrics.

Reconstruction quality is measured with PSNR, SSIM \cite{wang2004ssim}, mean absolute error (MAE), and high-percentile absolute error. For tiled runs, we additionally record a seam metric and inspect heatmaps. This combination matters because a configuration can preserve average PSNR and SSIM while producing regular boundary artifacts that are visually important.

\section{Implementation}

\subsection{CDC Path}

The implementation uses the x-parameterized CDC path adapted in this repository from the original CDC implementation \cite{yang2022lossy}. The x-parameterized path does not expose a direct runtime parameter named \texttt{compression\_level}, \texttt{quality}, \texttt{bitrate}, or \texttt{quantization}. Compression level is therefore controlled through checkpoint choice. The experiment suite reports checkpoint name together with measured BPP, compression ratio, PSNR, and SSIM so the compression setting is interpretable.

\subsection{Experiment Runner}

Each run writes a per-image \texttt{results.csv}, an aggregate \texttt{summary.csv}, a manifest, a short report, and optional visual files. The runner records wall time, GPU-timed inference time, peak GPU memory, BPP, compression ratio, PSNR, SSIM, MSE, RMSE, MAE, 95th and 99th percentile absolute error, maximum absolute error, bias, error standard deviation, and tiled-only seam error. The lightweight GitHub package stores tables and downsampled visuals. Full-resolution reconstructions, checkpoints, raw images, and full logs remain on DeltaAI.

\subsection{Tiling}

Native full-resolution decoding is memory-heavy because the full image tensor and diffusion intermediates must be represented together. Tiling splits the image into fixed-size patches, compresses and reconstructs each patch, and stitches the outputs back to the native image size. We evaluate $512 \times 512$, $1024 \times 1024$, and $2048 \times 2048$ in the first pilot, then add $256 \times 256$ for the selected run. Tiling changes the memory footprint, but it can introduce boundary artifacts. The workflow therefore saves three families of visual evidence:
\begin{itemize}
\item original/reconstruction/error comparison panels,
\item absolute-error heatmaps,
\item visual QA panels with histograms and difference distributions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Data}

The experiments use full-resolution drone images cropped to $5440 \times 3648$ pixels. The reconstruction step sweep uses a repeated profiling set. The tiling pilot uses 8 images. The selected tiling validation uses 50 images. The compression-side validation uses 20 images. These sample sizes are sufficient to identify runtime-memory regimes, but they are not yet a final large-scale benchmark. We therefore treat the results as an operating-point study.

\subsection{Platform}

The main experiments run on NCSA DeltaAI with GH200-class hardware. The committed results should be read as DeltaAI GH200 results unless a row explicitly states otherwise. A small Delta H200 check is included only as a platform-sensitivity note. It is not treated as a full cross-system benchmark.

\subsection{Experiment Matrix}

The experiment suite covers seven questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item How does denoising step count affect reconstruction time and quality?
\item Does fp16 reduce runtime and memory, and can it be used for BPP reporting?
\item How much do resolution reduction and native full-resolution decoding differ?
\item Does controlled-resolution batching improve throughput?
\item Which checkpoint provides the best compression-quality point?
\item Does tiling reduce memory without unacceptable quality loss?
\item Does local node storage improve runtime relative to shared storage?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Tiling Reduces Runtime and Memory}

The selected tiling validation compares no tiling, $256 \times 256$ tiling, and $512 \times 512$ tiling on 50 full-resolution images. Table~\ref{tab:tiling} summarizes the result. The $256 \times 256$ setting reduces time from 144.35 to 79.84 seconds per image and peak memory from 52.01 to 1.66 GB. Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:speedup} and \eqref{eq:memorygain}, this is a 44.7\% runtime reduction and a 31.3$\times$ peak-memory reduction relative to no tiling.

The $256 \times 256$ setting is also faster and smaller than $512 \times 512$ tiling. It is about 9.0\% faster and uses about 45.0\% less memory. \subsection{Resolution Reduction Is Fast but Quality-Limited}

Resolution reduction gives the largest raw speedup, but it changes the information available to downstream tasks. In the compression-side validation, reducing the longest edge to 4K lowers time from 144.32 to 28.89 seconds per image and peak memory from 52.00 to 28.98 GB. Reducing to 2K lowers time to 7.36 seconds and memory to 7.47 GB, but SSIM drops to 0.689. The 1K setting is faster again, but the quality loss is too large for detailed visual inspection unless a downstream task confirms that such coarse imagery is adequate.

\subsection{Batch Size Does Not Improve the Controlled 2K Run}

The controlled 2K batch-size sweep shows that larger batches do not improve throughput in the current implementation. Batch size 1 takes 7.35 seconds per image and uses 7.47 GB. The likely reason is that batching increases memory pressure without increasing effective throughput enough to offset overhead.

\subsection{Storage Placement Has Little Effect in the Current Run}

The storage comparison shows almost no difference between shared project filesystem access and node-local staged input. Shared storage takes 143.86 seconds per image, while local staging takes 143.89 seconds per image. This suggests that the tested native baseline is compute-bound rather than I/O-bound. This conclusion should not be generalized to all future runs: larger concurrent jobs or different filesystem pressure could change the result. For the current workflow, however, tiling and denoising-step control are higher-priority optimizations than local staging.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Main Operating Point}

The selected $256 \times 256$ tiling configuration is the strongest current operating point because it changes the most limiting systems quantity: memory. Moving from 52.01 GB to 1.66 GB peak memory changes how the workflow can be scheduled. It lowers the risk of out-of-memory failures, leaves room for larger surrounding pipelines, and makes concurrent processing more realistic. The runtime improvement is also meaningful: a 50-image validation run moves from about two hours at the no-tiling baseline to about 1.1 hours at the selected tiling setting, before considering queue and parallelization effects.

\subsection{Why Not Use Resolution Reduction Alone?}

Resolution reduction is tempting because it is fast. The 2K setting is roughly 20$\times$ faster than native full-resolution processing in the measured compression-side validation. However, it changes the visual information before compression. For disaster-response inspection, this can be unacceptable: small roof damage, debris lines, street-level obstructions, and building-edge features may disappear. The right question is therefore not "which setting is fastest?" but "which setting preserves the evidence needed by the next task?" Tiling is more attractive because it keeps the native image boundary available while reducing memory pressure.

\subsection{Compression Ratio Is Not Enough}

The checkpoint sweep shows why compression ratio cannot be reported alone. The \texttt{b00128} checkpoint gives a high compression ratio, but at lower SSIM. The \texttt{b00512} checkpoint gives stronger quality but lower compression. The correct checkpoint depends on whether the downstream user values storage savings, visual fidelity, or classification performance. A future version of this work should choose checkpoints using task-level metrics, not only PSNR and SSIM.

\subsection{Implications for Disaster-Response Workflows}

The disaster-imagery setting makes hallucination and loss of detail especially important. Generative visual systems can produce plausible outputs that do not preserve critical structural evidence \cite{yang2026satellite}. Damage-assessment models can also depend on subtle pre- and post-event differences \cite{yang2025hyperlocal}. A CDC workflow for this domain should therefore be conservative: it should expose heatmaps, preserve raw links to original data, and mark configurations that have not been validated with downstream tasks.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}

This draft has four main limitations. First, the selected validation uses 50 full-resolution images and the compression-side validation uses 20 images. These runs identify promising operating points, but they are not yet a statistically broad benchmark across many disaster scenes. Second, the visual QA panels show representative examples. A final study should inspect more images and report artifact frequencies. Third, the current metrics are image-level fidelity metrics, not downstream damage-assessment accuracy. The final recommendation should be tested with tasks such as road obstruction detection, building-damage classification, or human visual inspection. Fourth, the fp16 path is not ready for compression-ratio reporting because BPP values are invalid in the current outputs. This is an implementation issue, not evidence that fp16 is unusable.

\section{Future Work}

The next version of this paper should add three experiments. First, run the selected $256 \times 256$ and $512 \times 512$ settings on a larger sample and include artifact-frequency reporting. Second, evaluate downstream task performance using disaster-analysis tasks related to street-view and cross-view damage assessment. Third, separate model compression choices from systems choices by testing checkpoint selection under the same tiling and denoising-step settings.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper evaluates CDC as a cost-aware HPC workflow for high-resolution drone imagery. The central finding is practical: native full-image CDC is feasible on DeltaAI GH200, but it is too slow and memory-heavy to be the default operating point. Tiling changes the cost profile. On 50 full-resolution images, $256 \times 256$ tiling reduces runtime by 44.7\% and peak memory by 31.3$\times$ relative to no tiling, with small scalar quality losses relative to $512 \times 512$ tiling. Resolution reduction and checkpoint selection remain useful controls, but they should be chosen with downstream visual-evidence needs in mind. The broader lesson is that neural compression for disaster imagery should be evaluated as an end-to-end system: storage, runtime, memory, fidelity, and visual artifact evidence all belong in the same table.
